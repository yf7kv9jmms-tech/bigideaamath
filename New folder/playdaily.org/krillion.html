The Krillion Game, but Endless - Krillion Unlimited

Only uncommon answers score: one open prompt, one answer, and the whole ocean under you. The obvious answer barely moves you; the obscure one takes you to the trench floor. This is Krillion Unlimited: the Krillion game with no daily dive to wait for.

How to play the Krillion game

The rules, the tiers, and the questions people ask.

How to play

  1. 1A prompt appears and a 25-second clock starts. Type one answer.
  2. 2The obvious answer is worth almost nothing. The obscure one is worth everything.
  3. 3Every point sinks you 10 metres. 700 points is 7,000m: the trench floor, and a perfect dive.
  4. 4A wrong answer is refused, not punished. It costs you the seconds it took.

Questions

What is the Krillion game?

Krillion is a free browser game about giving the rarest correct answer. You get 7 open prompts, 25 seconds each, and one answer per prompt. Everybody can answer "Name a city in California". The game is about whether you say Los Angeles or Tehachapi. Rare answers sink you deeper into the ocean, and the depth is your score.

How is Krillion Unlimited different from the daily Krillion game?

Krillion Unlimited takes the daily game's one dive and stops rationing it. The original deals 7 prompts once a day and ends; here a refresh deals 7 completely new ones from a bank of thousands, as often as you like. Endless mode goes further and deals them one at a time forever, on 3 tanks of air, past the trench floor and down through 138 zones to 250km.

How is Krillion scored?

Every accepted answer lands in one of six tiers, worth 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 100 points: plankton, too clever, schooler, rare, deep cut, one in a krillion. Each point sinks you 10 metres, so a perfect 700-point dive puts you at 7,000m, on the trench floor. A refused answer scores nothing and costs only the time you spent on it.

What do Plankton, Too Clever, Schooler, Rare, Deep Cut and One in a Krillion mean?

They are the six tiers an accepted answer can land in, best last, and they are graded by how many other people would have said the same thing. Plankton is the answer everyone gives and moves you 40 metres. One in a Krillion is the answer nobody else reached and moves you 1,000 metres. The tier, not the clock, is the whole score.

How do you get a good score in the Krillion game?

Go specific, then go sideways. The answer that feels clever to you probably feels clever to a few thousand other people, so the points live in the corner cases: the smallest valid example, the one from a country nobody thinks of, the technically-correct edge of the category. Answering fast is worth nothing on its own. The clock only decides whether you answer at all.

What is Endless mode?

One prompt at a time, forever, with 3 tanks of air. A miss costs a tank; a one-in-a-krillion answer gives one back. The prompts get harder the deeper you are, and below the real trench the ocean keeps going through 108 hand-written zones, past drowned cities, vent fields, a glass orchard and a machine nobody built a room for, down to a shrine at 250km. A hundred prompts will not get you there.

Can you play Krillion multiplayer?

Yes. Press Play multiplayer on the start screen and pick a live room of up to 30 players, or start your own. Everyone gets the same prompt at the same moment, with 25 seconds to answer. After each prompt you see what everyone said and how many points it scored, and the deepest diver at the end wins. No host, no sign-up.

Is the Krillion game free, and how often can I play?

Krillion is free, and you can play as often as you like. There is no sign-up, no app and no daily cap: refresh and it deals 7 more prompts. It runs in the browser, so it plays the same on a phone as on a laptop.

Is there an archive of past Krillion games?

Krillion Unlimited has no archive because it has no daily dive to file away. Every refresh deals 7 new prompts from a bank of thousands, so there is always a dive you have not played, and Endless mode keeps dealing for as long as your air lasts.

How deep does the ocean actually go in Krillion?

The first 11,000 metres are real: the kelp line, the point sunlight stops, the Titanic at 3,800m, anglerfish territory, the hadal zone named for Hades, and Challenger Deep at 10,935m. Past that the map is invented and hand-drawn, 138 zones in all, ending at 250km. Nothing about it is procedurally generated. Somebody wrote every one.

The game in a sentence

What is Krillion?

Krillion is a rarest-answer trivia game: every prompt has many right answers, and the one that fewest people would give is worth the most.

Most quizzes ask what you know. The Krillion game asks what you know that other people do not. A prompt such as "Name a bird" is open, so a hundred answers are correct, but they are not equal: the answer on everyone's lips barely moves you, and an obscure answer that is still right sends you to the sea floor. It is general knowledge played against the crowd, not against a question setter.

The word

What does krillion mean?

A krillion is not a real number. It is a pun with a small crustacean inside it.

English has a family of invented big numbers, zillion, jillion and gazillion among them, which the linguist Stephen Chrisomalis has named indefinite hyperbolic numerals. They borrow the ending of million and billion to mean more than anyone could count. Krillion swaps in krill, and the phrase it belongs to is a play on one in a million. It comes from the 2011 animated film Happy Feet Two, directed by George Miller, where Will the Krill, voiced by Brad Pitt, leaves his swarm and announces: "I am one in a krillion."

The joke works because of what krill are. Antarctic krill, Euphausia superba, grow to about six centimetres and gather in swarms that can reach 30,000 animals to the cubic metre. Their combined weight is estimated at about 379 million tonnes, among the largest totals for any single wild animal species, and they rise towards the surface at night and sink by day. The word itself is Norwegian, for the small fry of fish. To be one in a krillion is to be the single krill that did something different from the swarm, which is exactly what the Krillion game pays for.

A short history

Where the rarest-answer quiz comes from

The format is an old one turned upside down.

  1. 1969

    Psychologists count the answers

    William Battig and William Montague ask hundreds of American students to list members of 56 categories, "a bird", "a fruit", and publish how often each answer was given, replicating and extending an American study of 1957. Their category norms are the best-known rarity tables in psychology.

  2. 1976

    The survey quiz

    Family Feud goes on air in the United States. A hundred people are surveyed and contestants win by naming the most popular answer. Britain's version, Family Fortunes, follows in 1980.

  3. 1988

    Duplicates score nothing

    The party game Scattergories makes originality the rule at the table: an answer another player also wrote down earns no point.

  4. 2009

    The survey, inverted

    The BBC quiz Pointless keeps the hundred-person survey and flips the aim: the fewer of the hundred who gave your answer, the better, and an answer none of them gave is a pointless answer, the best there is.

  5. Now

    The crowd is everyone

    The Krillion game drops the panel of a hundred and grades an answer on how familiar it is to people at large, which is why one prompt can run from the first answer anyone gives to one that almost nobody reaches.

Why it works

Why is everyone's first answer the same answer?

Ask a room to name a bird and most of the room says the same two or three birds. Cognitive psychology has known why for fifty years.

  • Categories have favourites

    Eleanor Rosch's prototype theory, from the 1970s, showed that categories are built around best examples. A robin is a better bird than a penguin in everybody's head, so it is named first and named by everyone.

  • Easy to recall feels right

    Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman named the availability heuristic in 1973: what comes to mind most easily gets treated as the most likely and the most important. In this game the most available answer is the cheapest one.

  • The tail is long

    In the counted norms, a few answers take most of the votes and hundreds of correct ones are each given by almost nobody. In one British study of 2022, more than half of all the different answers collected were given by a single person. Every prompt has that shape: a crowded head and a long, quiet tail where the points are.

The trap in the middle

Why the Too Clever answer is not the rare one

The second tier from the bottom is there to punish the answer that feels original and is not.

Once players know the obvious answer is worthless, they all skip it and land on the same second thought. The economist Thomas Schelling described this in The Strategy of Conflict in 1960: people who cannot confer still converge on focal points, the choices that seem natural to everyone. John Maynard Keynes made the same observation about markets in 1936 with his newspaper beauty contest, where the winning move is to guess what average opinion expects average opinion to be.

A rarest-answer game is that contest in reverse. You are trying not to meet the crowd, and the crowd is trying not to meet you, so the clever answer fills up fast. The real rarities lie a step further out: the specialist's example, the regional one, the member of the category that is correct and that nobody thinks of as typical.

The method

How to find the uncommon answer in 25 seconds

Four habits that work on any prompt in Krillion Unlimited.

  1. 01

    Say the obvious answer, then bin it

    Your first answer is the prototype. Naming it to yourself clears it out of the way and tells you what the crowd will say.

  2. 02

    Bin the second one too

    The answer that arrives next is usually the focal point for everyone who thought the same way you just did.

  3. 03

    Change the angle, not the effort

    Rare answers come from a different slice of the category: another country, another century, a trade you have worked in, the smallest or oldest member.

  4. 04

    Stay inside the prompt

    An answer that is not accepted scores nothing, so obscure only pays when it is unarguably correct. Pick the rarest answer you are sure of, not the rarest you can imagine.

The real ocean

How deep is a perfect Krillion dive?

700 points is 7,000 m, and the water on the way down is mapped by oceanographers into five zones.

Sunlight zone, to 200 m
The epipelagic. Enough light for photosynthesis, and home to most of the life people picture when they think of the sea.
Twilight zone, 200 to 1,000 m
The mesopelagic. Light fades to nothing. Vast numbers of animals shelter here by day and climb at night to feed, the largest daily migration on Earth.
Midnight zone, 1,000 to 4,000 m
The bathypelagic. No sunlight at all, near-freezing water, and the light that exists is made by animals.
Abyssal zone, 4,000 to 6,000 m
The abyssopelagic, over the great flat plains that make up most of the ocean floor.
Hadal zone, below 6,000 m
The ocean trenches, for the most part. The Danish oceanographer Anton Bruun proposed the name in 1956, after Hades, the Greek underworld.
Challenger Deep, about 10,935 m
The deepest known point of the ocean, in the Mariana Trench of the western Pacific.

A perfect classic dive ends 1,000 m into the hadal zone. Endless mode in Krillion Unlimited keeps going, past the depth of any real trench, into an ocean that was drawn for the game.

In this game

How Krillion Unlimited turns rarity into depth

An accepted answer lands in one of 6 tiers according to how many other people would have given it, from Plankton, the answer everyone gives, to One in a Krillion, the one nobody else reached. Speed earns nothing: the clock only decides whether you answer at all. A refused answer costs the seconds it took and no more, so a reach is always worth trying. There is no daily cap either: a new dive is one refresh away.
